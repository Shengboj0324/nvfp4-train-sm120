# Quantizer fixes beat kernel heroics on RTX 50

The highest-value work for an sm_120a NVFP4 training library in November 2026 is in the quantizer, not the GEMM or attention kernels. The current best published recipe, Mosaic NVFP4 ("What Matters for NVFP4 Training?", Oct 2026), differs from this library's NVIDIA-2025-style recipe in four ways. It uses 1D weight scales with backward re-quantization instead of 16x16 blocks. It applies "Four Over Six" (4/6) adaptive block scaling to every round-to-nearest tensor. It replaces element-wise stochastic rounding (SR) on gradients with MS-EDEN, which is unbiased and has more than 2x lower error. And it applies a 128-wide randomized Hadamard transform (RHT) to both backward GEMMs. Together these cut the gap to BF16 by 15–25% in Quartet II's 0.5–2B runs and by 0.24–0.36 percentage points at 8–30B. MS-EDEN also largely dissolves problem (4): it needs SR only on one E4M3 scale per 16 elements. Until it lands, an integer-domain 8-bit rounder cuts the software SR cost from about 28.6 to 10–12 extra SASS instructions per element. For attention (2), the two-term P·V is already the cheapest published way to keep Delta consistent. Attn-QAT pays a 1.2–2x training slowdown for the same idea, so the remaining lever is softmax and exp arithmetic, not a different backward. The GEMM gap (3) shows up only at locked clocks; at the power limit the kernel already matches or beats cuBLASLt. Its documented fix, three MMA warps per scheduler, is real but low-yield in practice. MoE grouped GEMM (5) has no consumer-GPU training prior art at all, so it is a 4–6-week build, not a port. One caveat governs everything that follows: the library's benchmark (GPT-124M, 49M tokens, about 0.4 tokens per parameter) is far below every published study's horizon. The first step is therefore to make the benchmark able to rank recipes.

## The 1.28% gap is a forward-error problem the 2026 recipes already shrink

The library implements NVIDIA's September 2025 recipe almost exactly. That recipe uses 1x16 E4M3 block scales with an FP32 tensor scale on activations and gradients, **16x16 2D blocks on weights** so that fprop and dgrad see identical values, RHT16 on the wgrad operands only, SR on gradients, round-to-nearest-even elsewhere, and the last 2 of 12 blocks in BF16 ([DESIGN.md §2](../../DESIGN.md)). That is NVIDIA's published configuration: under 15% of linears in high precision, 16x16 RHT on wgrad, 2D weight scaling, and SR only on gradients ([NVIDIA, arXiv 2509.25149](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.25149v2)). The measured gap is **+0.0191 ± 0.0016 nats (+1.28%) across three seeds**, constant over training. All-blocks-NVFP4 costs only 0.002 more, and the wgrad RHT is unmeasurable at this scale ([RESULTS.md §4](../../RESULTS.md)). A constant gap that barely responds to tail-layer precision points at per-step representation error in the forward pass, not at drift that accumulates.

The literature agrees on where that error lives. NVIDIA's 12B run found that switching only the forward pass to BF16 late in training **cuts the relative loss error from 1.5% to 0.5%** ([NVIDIA](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.25149v2)). TetraJet-v2's sensitivity analysis at 370M attributes **+0.43 of a +0.83 perplexity gap to forward activations alone**. It also shows that keeping the FFN down-projection (fc2) in BF16 recovers 0.35, more than any attention module ([TetraJet-v2, arXiv 2510.27527](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.27527v1)). Forward-side fixes therefore come first.

**4/6 is the cheapest forward fix, but it does nothing for 16x16 weights.** For each 16-element block, the quantizer encodes twice, mapping the block maximum to 6 and to 4, and keeps the encoding with lower MSE. The FP32 tensor scale constant changes from 448 to 256 so the larger scale still fits in E4M3. The overhead is under 15% of the quantize op ([Four Over Six, arXiv 2512.02010](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.02010v3)). On a 30B-A3B / 1T-token run it brings training loss 22.3% closer to BF16 ([4/6](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.02010v3)). At small scale the gain is smaller: Quartet II measured gaps of 1.56% → 1.51% at 560M and 1.92% → 1.76% at 1.9B. Its ablation also found the benefit **roughly twice as large with native 1x16 scales as with square blocks** ([Quartet II, arXiv 2601.22813](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.22813)). The independent MSE measurements on N(0,1) explain why. 1x16 RTN gives 9.0e-3 and 1x16 RTN+4/6 gives 7.6e-3, while 16x16 gives 12.4e-3 with or without 4/6 ([Quartet II](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.22813)). Adding 4/6 to the library's 16x16 weights would therefore help activations only.

**The weight fix is 1D scales plus re-quantization, not 2D blocks.** The library's 16x16 weight blocks are the conservative choice NVIDIA validated, but 1x16+4/6 has **39% less weight MSE** (7.6 vs 12.4e-3). TetraJet-v2 measured 1x16 weights beating 16x16 at 150M (31.49 vs 31.74 perplexity) ([TetraJet-v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.27527v1)). Quartet II and Mosaic keep fprop/dgrad consistency a different way. They quantize W along K for fprop, then dequantize that already-quantized copy, transpose it, and re-quantize it for dgrad, so the two GEMMs see nearly the same values without paying square-block resolution ([Quartet II](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.22813); [Mosaic, arXiv 2610.24343](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.24343v1)). Weights are quantized once per optimizer step, so the extra pass is almost free in this library's weight-cache design.

**MS-EDEN replaces gradient SR and is the largest single gain in the newest recipe.** MS-EDEN works on 128-element chunks. It applies an RHT with a shared seed, so the rotations cancel in the GEMM inner dimension, and then rounds to NVFP4 with round-to-nearest using a slightly clipped grid and an FP8 scale cap of 256. For each 16-element group it computes a correction S = ⟨x, x⟩ / ⟨x, x_RTN⟩ and folds it into the E4M3 group scale **using SR on the scale only**. The result is unbiased in expectation, with MSE 9.4e-3 against 23.5e-3 for element-wise SR ([Quartet II](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.22813)). Quartet II's full stack (MS-EDEN in backward, 4/6 forward, 1x16 + re-quant) reached gaps of **1.26% vs NVIDIA's 1.56% at 560M and 1.44% vs 1.92% at 1.9B**. Its kernels run on an RTX 5090 at more than 4x BF16 linear-layer speed ([Quartet II](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.22813)), so an sm_120 reference implementation exists. Mosaic adds dim-128 tiled RHT on **both** wgrad and dgrad with resampled signs, and 4/6 on X^T and W^T. It reports **"MS-EDEN + 4/6 on backward tensors" as the largest single gain**, landing 0.36 pp better than NVIDIA-25 on Nemotron 3 Nano and 0.24 pp better on an 8B model ([Mosaic](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.24343v1)).

Two conflicts constrain the details. First, Quartet II reports that **4/6 combined with SR introduces bias**, while the 4/6 authors used them together without flagging it ([Quartet II](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.22813) vs [4/6](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.02010v3)). The safe rule is to apply 4/6 only to round-to-nearest tensors. Second, dgrad RHT gives contradictory results across papers. NVIDIA found it harmful with RHT16 and a fixed seed. TetraJet-v2 (RHT32, dX+dW best at 28.42 vs 28.47 perplexity for dW-only) and Mosaic (dim-128, resampled signs) found it helpful ([NVIDIA](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.25149v2); [TetraJet-v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.27527v1); [Mosaic](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.24343v1)). The consistent reading is that dgrad RHT helps when it is wide, re-randomized and paired with an unbiased gradient quantizer, which is exactly the MS-EDEN configuration. Still, this library needs to test it, not assume it.

| Mosaic element | Library today | Change needed |
|---|---|---|
| 1D weights + backward re-quantization | 16x16 2D weights | Replace the 2D path in the weight cache |
| 4/6 on W, X (fwd) and X^T, W^T (bwd) | none | Dual-candidate quantize, tensor constant 448 → 256 |
| MS-EDEN on gradients | element-wise SR, 16 bits per element | RHT128 + RTN + per-16 correction with SR on scales |
| dim-128 RHT on wgrad and dgrad, resampled signs | RHT16 on wgrad only | Wider transform, applied to dgrad operands too |
| last ~15% of layers BF16 | last 2 of 12 blocks (≈17%) | none |

Several larger levers are documented but cost more or fit this regime worse. Full-Stack FP4's BF16 low-rank SVD subspace cut a 1B gap from **1.40% to 0.61%**, at +4.3% parameters and extra BF16 GEMMs ([Full-Stack FP4, arXiv 2607.04422](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.04422v2)). TetraJet-v2's OsciReset plus FP8 outlier channels reported about 51% gap reduction, but only in simulation ([TetraJet-v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.27527v1)). AMD's weight de-oscillation hook halved an MXFP4 gap at 20B ([ALTO, arXiv 2610.32229](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.32229v2)). Oscillation fixes target late training, though, and this library's gap is flat from the first evaluation. UFP4's uniform grid beats E2M1 by about 0.3 pp, but it cannot run on NVFP4 tensor cores ([UFP4, arXiv 2606.20381](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.20381v1)).

The horizon caveat is serious. Mosaic found that **recipe rankings do not stabilize until well past 150B tokens** at 8B, and weaker recipes can look better early ([Mosaic](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.24343v1)). The smallest published ablations run at about 25 tokens per parameter ([Quartet II](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.22813)), roughly 60x this benchmark's density. The library's seed-to-seed standard deviation of 0.0016 nats ([RESULTS.md §4.1](../../RESULTS.md)) means each ablation needs three seeds to resolve a 0.003-nat (15%) improvement. One run at ≥20 tokens per parameter (about 2.5B tokens, roughly 9 hours at the measured 80k tokens/s) should confirm the winner before it becomes the default. Applying the published relative reductions to 1.28% suggests that 4/6, 1D re-quantization and MS-EDEN together could reach roughly 0.9–1.1%. That figure is an extrapolation, not a prediction this regime has validated.

## Software stochastic rounding drops to ten instructions, then mostly disappears

Hardware help is not coming to sm_120. In PTX ISA 9.5 the `.rs` rounding mode and every `cvt.rs` form are restricted to **sm_100a, sm_103a and sm_107a**, with no family targets. The newer `.rz` FP4 converts are gated to sm_107f and above. sm_120a only has round-to-nearest `cvt.rn.satfinite.e2m1x2` ([PTX ISA, cvt](https://docs.nvidia.com/cuda/parallel-thread-execution/index.html#data-movement-and-conversion-instructions-cvt)). Transformer Engine reached the same conclusion: its FP4 cast falls back to a software rounder on architectures without `cvt.rs` ([TE ptx.cuh](https://github.com/NVIDIA/TransformerEngine/blob/main/transformer_engine/common/util/ptx.cuh)).

**The library spends twice the industry's random-bit budget, and computes the rounding expensively.** NVIDIA's hardware SR feeds **8 random bits per E2M1 element**. TE and torchao draw one Philox4x32-10 `generate4()` per 16 elements and hand one 32-bit word to each four-element convert ([PTX ISA](https://docs.nvidia.com/cuda/parallel-thread-execution/index.html#data-movement-and-conversion-instructions-cvt); [TE core_nvfp4.cuh](https://github.com/NVIDIA/TransformerEngine/blob/main/transformer_engine/common/cast/nvfp4/core_nvfp4.cuh); [torchao CuTe DSL kernels](https://github.com/pytorch/ao/blob/main/torchao/prototype/moe_training/nvfp4_training/_cutedsl_kernels_impl.py)). The library hashes once per pair for 16 bits per element ([DESIGN.md §3.2](../../DESIGN.md)). Fewer bits are only safe with the right offset. Graphcore's analysis shows naive N-bit SR ("SRFF") has a systematic bias of −2^-(N+1) ulp toward zero, and adding half an LSB ("SRF") removes it for free. In a 354M GPT-2-scale test with 3 random bits, SRFF ended at loss 4.06 against 3.14 for SRF ([Fitzgibbon & Felix, arXiv 2504.20634](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.20634v1)). This research's exhaustive check found that TE's own software fallback is statistically SRFF: mean bias −0.00196 step. An integer-domain SRF rounder has mean bias exactly zero and per-value bias at most 2^-9 step ([bias_check.py](code/bias_check.py)).

Static SASS counts from 16-element-per-thread probe kernels, compiled with CUDA 13.0 for sm_120a, show where the cost goes ([sr_variants.cu](code/sr_variants.cu)).

| Variant (RNG + rounding math) | Extra SASS / element over RN |
|---|---|
| Current library: pair hash, 16-bit uniforms, float-domain SR | **28.6** (≈9.5 RNG + ≈19 math) |
| TE's software path: Philox4x32-10 + float SR | 24.8 |
| One lowbias32 per 4 elements (8 bits) + integer SR | 11.9 |
| Philox4x32-7 per 16 elements + integer SR | 11.4 |
| pcg4d per 16 elements + integer SR | **10.4** |

The integer rounder adds 8 random bits on top of the 22 discarded FP32 mantissa bits, plus a half-LSB offset, and then masks. That leaves an exact E2M1 grid value, which the hardware RN convert passes through unchanged. Values below 1 are lifted into [1, 2), where one kept mantissa bit matches E2M1's 0.5 spacing ([sr_variants.cu](code/sr_variants.cu)):

```cuda
__device__ __forceinline__ float int_sr(float x, uint32_t r8) {   // x already divided by the block scale
  const float a = fabsf(x);
  const float off = a < 1.0f ? 1.0f : 0.0f;
  const float z = a + off;
  uint32_t b = __float_as_uint(z) + ((r8 & 0xFFu) << 14) + (1u << 13);  // 8 random bits + 1/2 LSB (SRF)
  b &= 0xFFC00000u;                                                     // keep sign/exponent/1 mantissa bit
  return copysignf(fminf(__uint_as_float(b) - off, 6.0f), x);           // then exact cvt.rn.satfinite.e2m1x2
}
```

For RNG quality, pcg4d sits on Jarzynski and Olano's speed/quality Pareto frontier for GPU hashes ([JCGT 2020](https://jcgt.org/published/0009/03/02/paper.pdf)), and Philox4x32-7 is the minimum round count verified Crush-resistant ([Random123, SC11](https://users.cs.utah.edu/~hari/teaching/bigdata/random123sc11.pdf)). Cutting from 28.6 to 10–12 instructions per element should shrink the measured +34–43% dual-quantizer overhead to roughly +12–18%, if the overhead tracks ALU work. That is an estimate from static counts and still needs a wall-clock measurement. Going below 8 bits is not worth it: 4 bits saves about one more instruction per element but widens the never-rounds-up dead zone to 1/32 of a step ([Fitzgibbon & Felix](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.20634v1)). Shared or fixed noise patterns are a trap. S3R found that sharing one threshold per token "degrades convergence" unless explicitly corrected ([S3R, arXiv 2610.36587](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36587)).

The integer rounder is a two-day bridge. The structural answer is MS-EDEN: once gradients use RTN after RHT128 with SR only on the per-16 scale, element-wise RNG falls from 16 draws to 1 per block ([Quartet II](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.22813)). The new cost is the wider Hadamard and one dot-product ratio per group. UFP4 measured a fused RHT16+quantize at only 1.06–1.07x the plain quantize ([UFP4](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.20381v1)), but no one has published the cost of RHT128 on sm_120. The integer rounder also serves MS-EDEN's scale SR, with the random field shifted to E4M3's three mantissa bits.

## Two-term P·V is already the cheapest consistent backward

Every training-grade low-precision attention makes the backward's Delta = rowsum(dO∘O) equal to rowsum(P∘dP) for the P the backward actually uses, and the published methods differ only in how. **Attn-QAT** stores a high-precision O′ = ΣP̃V alongside the quantized O. That costs about +50% forward FLOPs and a **1.2–2x training slowdown** ([Attn-QAT, arXiv 2603.00040](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.00040v3)). **FLAMIA** writes its own backward that recomputes P bit-identically to the forward (same quantized Q/K, tiling, exp and scaling) and forms D with the same quantized dO. Any mismatch caused divergence ([FLAMIA, arXiv 2611.03344](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.03344)). **Backward-State Policy** shows that reusing the forward's rounded O fails outright (loss 7.31 vs 3.26 at 390M). It also shows that an FP8 forward with a BF16 backward fed the unrounded O lands **0.003–0.006 nats above BF16** ([Backward-State Policy, arXiv 2609.39813](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.39813)).

The library's v0.10 design computes e4m3(P)V plus a scaled e4m3 residual term, carrying P to about 2^-8. It then runs PyTorch's BF16 flash backward on the dequantized operands. That is the Attn-QAT idea at a much lower price: a quarter of the forward's speedup (1.5–2.1x → 1.2–1.6x), with a net 1.07–1.12x over SDPA for forward plus backward. The quality gap at 4096 tokens is gone, at −0.09% ([DESIGN.md §7.1](../../DESIGN.md)). The repo has also established that the cost is **the extra MMA itself, not the residual arithmetic** ([DESIGN.md §7.1](../../DESIGN.md)). That rules out cheapening the residual's ALU work. It also kills a 4-bit residual: FP4 operands in 8-bit containers (`kind::f8f6f4`) run on the same pipe at the same rate as FP8 (517.8 TFLOP/s on an RTX PRO 6000 Max-Q, against 988 for packed block-scaled FP4), so only packed FP4 on *both* operands gets the 2x rate ([waynehacking8 kernels](https://github.com/waynehacking8/blackwell-tensorcore-kernels)).

The alternatives are worse trades. A FLAMIA-style backward would recover the forward's 25%, but the library would need its own BF16 flash backward at PyTorch-FA2 speed, a large build. The repo's own 8-bit backward ran no faster than FA2's (≈80 TFLOPS each, bound by element-wise work) ([DESIGN.md §7.1](../../DESIGN.md)). Backward-State Policy also argues that using rounded P in the softmax backward is biased, which contradicts FLAMIA's approach ([Backward-State Policy](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.39813)). Settling that disagreement would take a dedicated experiment. The ceiling matters too: the forward is roughly a third of attention's training time, so removing the extra MMA entirely would lift attention from about 1.1x to about 1.15–1.2x SDPA. That is worth a few percent of end-to-end step time on long-context models and almost nothing on GPT-124M at 512 tokens.

Two cheaper experiments remain. The first is **FlashAttention-4's polynomial exp2**. It moves 10–25% of each row's exponentials to a degree-3 Cody-Waite/Horner evaluation on the FMA pipe, within one BF16 ulp of hardware on 99% of inputs ([FA4, arXiv 2603.05451](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.05451)). It needs no tcgen05 hardware and targets the exposed softmax arithmetic the repo measured: 8-bit kernels at about 45% of tensor peak ([DESIGN.md §7](../../DESIGN.md)). Its payoff depends on the sm_120 MUFU.EX2 rate, which has not been published. Lazy rescaling is already at its limit. The library quantizes P as e4m3(2^7·p) and rescales when the row max grows by more than 1.5, so c + τ = 8.5. FLAMIA's saturation bound is c + τ ≤ log2 448 ≈ 8.8 ([FLAMIA](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.03344)), so adopting FA4's τ = 8 would mean giving up P resolution. The second experiment is a **single-term INT8 P·V**. SageBwd's forward uses INT8 P with a per-token rowmax/127 scale, and on sm_120 the INT8 MMA rate (473 TFLOPS in the repo's measurements) is above block-scaled E4M3 (438) ([SageBwd, arXiv 2603.02170](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.02170)). Running it through the existing `attention_qbwd_probe.py` would show whether its uniform absolute error keeps dQ near the two-term's 1.2–2.0%. Diffuse long-context attention favours relative-error formats, so expect it to lose, but the test costs days, not weeks.

If an 8-bit backward comes back later, the published guidance is specific. Keep dP = dO·V^T in 16-bit, because dQ cosine falls from 99.77% to 97.47% when dP is INT8 ([SageAttention3, arXiv 2505.11594](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.11594v3)). Expect to need QK-norm or small batches for pretraining: SageBwd's 325M gap of 0.054 at 2.1M tokens/step vanishes at 260K ([SageBwd](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.02170)). The 2026 all-MXFP8 backwards (Meta's LP-FA4, FLAMIA, cuDNN) all target tcgen05. None has been ported to mma.sync ([LP-FA4 blog](https://pytorch.org/blog/low-precision-flash-attention-4-end-to-end-block-scaled-attention-for-blackwell/); [FLAMIA](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.03344)).

## The GEMM gap lives at locked clocks, and MoE has no consumer prior art

### Three MMA warps per scheduler is the documented route past 71%

The best published sm_120 NVFP4 GEMM is Colfax's CuTe DSL kernel on an RTX PRO 6000. It reaches **1666 TFLOP/s, 83% of nominal peak**. Its largest structural gain came from moving **from 8 to 12 MMA warps (two to three per scheduler) on a 192x128 tile at constant per-warp work**, even though stages dropped from 4 to 2. A 16-wide threadblock swizzle added another **+387 TFLOP/s at 32k**. Fixing scale-factor bank conflicts or trimming SF lane replication gave nothing ([Colfax SM120 optimization](https://research.colfax-intl.com/optimizing-an-nvfp4-blockscaled-gemm-on-rtx-pro-6000-blackwell-gpu-sm120/)). The library's kernel matches Colfax's starting point exactly: 128x128 tiles and 8 MMA warps, each owning a 64x32 tile of 2048 accumulators ([DESIGN.md §4](../../DESIGN.md)). Colfax's per-warp work is also 2048 accumulators (128x128 ÷ 8 and 192x128 ÷ 12). The repo's diagnosis is that ptxas spaces each warp's OMMAs 16 cycles apart, so with two warps per scheduler any stall idles the tensor pipe ([DESIGN.md §4.2](../../DESIGN.md)). That is precisely the condition a third warp fixes. Microbenchmarks agree: OMMA's dependent latency is only about 29 cycles ([sass-king](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/florianmattana/sass-king/main/knowledge/FINDINGS.md)), so raw issue capacity is not the limit; uncovered stalls are.

The blocker is registers, not the idea. Twelve MMA warps at 384 threads cap each thread at about 168 registers. The repo's 64x32 warp tile with double-buffered fragments needs 245, and it spilled the last time a third warp shared a scheduler ([DESIGN.md §4.1](../../DESIGN.md)). CUTLASS's sm_120 collective prefetches only one k-block of fragments ahead, with scale factors loaded by plain vectorized LDS ([CUTLASS sm120_blockscaled_mma_tma.hpp](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/NVIDIA/cutlass/main/include/cutlass/gemm/collective/sm120_blockscaled_mma_tma.hpp); [builder](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/NVIDIA/cutlass/main/include/cutlass/gemm/collective/builders/sm120_blockscaled_mma_builder.inl)). With a third warp hiding the 33-cycle LDSM latency, single-buffering is the trade to try. `setmaxnreg` works on sm_120a and both CUTLASS and Colfax use it (producer 40, MMA 232) ([CCCL setmaxnreg](https://nvidia.github.io/cccl/unstable/libcudacxx/ptx/instructions/setmaxnreg.html)). In this library, ptxas drops it because taking the address of a `__grid_constant__` tensor map inserts a call. Moving the descriptors to global memory is the documented prerequisite ([DESIGN.md §4.2](../../DESIGN.md)).

The case for doing this soon is weak. **At the power limit the gap does not exist**: on a desktop RTX 5090 the kernel runs at 1.03–1.71x cuBLASLt on every benchmarked shape ([DESIGN.md §6](../../DESIGN.md)). Locked-clock tensor-pipe busy is a proxy that pays off only on parts or clocks where the kernel is issue-bound rather than power-bound. Two cheap steps come first. Dumping the SASS of the kernel cuBLASLt actually picks (`cuobjdump -sass` on libcublasLt) answers what no public source does. Adding threadblock swizzle costs a day, though at GPT-124M widths its gain is likely small. Also check that nvcc 13.x does not spill more than 12.8 on sm_120, which one forum report claims ([NVIDIA forum](https://forums.developer.nvidia.com/t/nvcc-13-2-spilling-many-registers-for-cc-120/364169)).

### An sm_120 NVFP4 MoE path has to be built, and four kernels suffice

**No system ships an sm_120 NVFP4 MoE training path.** DeepGEMM and SonicMoE target SM90/SM100. torchao's NVFP4 MoE recipe targets B200, and its MXFP8 MoE is gated to SM100+ ([DeepGEMM](https://github.com/deepseek-ai/DeepGEMM); [SonicMoE](https://tridao.me/blog/2026/sonicmoe-blackwell/); [torchao #3293](https://github.com/pytorch/ao/issues/3293); [torchao moe_training](https://github.com/pytorch/ao/tree/main/torchao/prototype/moe_training)). CUTLASS added sm_120 NVFP4/MX grouped kernels to its profiler only in 4.4.2 (March 2026). Its 4.9 operator API with per-expert NVFP4 global scales and MoE wgrad does not say whether it covers SM120 ([CUTLASS CHANGELOG](https://github.com/NVIDIA/cutlass/blob/main/CHANGELOG.md)). Inference stacks hit garbage output and segfaults on sm_120 grouped FP4 for much of 2026. In one report, building for `compute_120f` instead of `120a` raised throughput from 14.6 to 39 tok/s ([FlashInfer #2723](https://github.com/flashinfer-ai/flashinfer/issues/2723)).

Every production system converged on the same structure. Tokens are sorted into expert-contiguous segments with a device-side offsets tensor and no host sync. Forward and dgrad run as M-grouped "2D x 3D" GEMMs, and wgrad runs as a K-grouped "2D x 2D" GEMM over tokens ([PyTorch MXFP8 MoE blog](https://pytorch.org/blog/mxfp8-training-for-moes-1-3x-training-speedup-vs-bf16-for-llama4-scout-on-gb200-cluster-using-torchao-and-torchtitan/); [DeepGEMM](https://github.com/deepseek-ai/DeepGEMM)). Segments are padded to 128 rows because the block-scaled scale layout tiles in 128x4 blocks, and wgrad is consistently the least efficient GEMM: 52% of peak in torchao ([torchao README](https://github.com/pytorch/ao/tree/main/torchao/prototype/moe_training)). The minimal sm_120 design therefore needs four kernels. The first is a fused permute-plus-quantize that reads X once by sorted index and writes FP4 data plus swizzled per-group scales, accumulating per-expert amax along the way, as miniTE does during dispatch ([miniTE](https://lmsys.org/blog/2026-10-25-minite)). The second is the existing dense TMA kernel driven by a device-built (expert, m_tile, n_tile) list that skips all-padding tiles. The third is a separate gather-and-sum combine kernel; SonicMoE found fusing scatter into the epilogue cost about 20% of TFLOPS ([SonicMoE](https://tridao.me/blog/2026/sonicmoe-blackwell/)). The fourth is wgrad, first in BF16 through `torch.nn.functional.grouped_mm` (SM80+) ([PyTorch grouped_mm](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/2.14/generated/torch.nn.functional.grouped_mm.html)).

Expectations should be modest. CUTLASS's own NVFP4 grouped GEMM averages only **1.11–1.12x** over torch's kernels on B200. miniTE's best MoE result, **1.33–1.37x**, kept the backward in BF16 ([CUTLASS CHANGELOG](https://github.com/NVIDIA/cutlass/blob/main/CHANGELOG.md); [miniTE](https://lmsys.org/blog/2026-10-25-minite)). torchao warns that quantization overhead can match the GEMM speedup at small dimensions ([PyTorch DeepSeek-V3 blog](https://pytorch.org/blog/enabling-up-to-41-faster-pre-training-mxfp8-and-deepep-for-deepseek-v3-on-b200-with-torchtitan/)), and a single consumer GPU means small per-expert M. MoE is a coverage feature, not a speed win, and it belongs last.

The implementation order follows the table, with three gates. **Week 1** starts with the harness (step 1). Build the integer rounder (step 2) next. It is independent, it lands a speed win in two days, and its scale-rounding variant is reused by MS-EDEN; update `reference.py` to the 8-bit SRF rule so the bit-exact tests still hold. Ship steps 3–5 in the same week as flags, and launch their three-seed runs in the background while the next phase is coded. The first gate: keep 1D-plus-4/6 only if it beats 16x16 by more than 2σ, because the evidence for that switch at 0.4 tokens per parameter is indirect. **Weeks 2–4** go to MS-EDEN with RHT128 on both backward GEMMs (step 6), using Quartet II's RTX 5090 kernels as the reference. Keep 4/6 off any tensor that still uses SR. The second gate is the long run: whichever forward/backward stack wins on short runs must also win at ≥20 tokens per parameter before it becomes the default. **Week 5** covers the two attention experiments (steps 7–8), each a few days and each falsifiable with tools the repo already has. The GEMM work (steps 9–10) and MoE (step 12) come after that. The third gate decides whether to start step 10 at all: begin it only when a target GPU is issue-bound at real clocks, which the laptop and the desktop 5090 are not.

## Conclusion

Every documented accuracy gain since NVIDIA's 2025 recipe works by spending a few more ALU instructions per quantized block to reduce error or bias: a second scale candidate, a re-quantization, a correction factor rounded into the scale. On sm_120, where the GEMM already outruns cuBLASLt under power limits, those instructions are cheap. MS-EDEN connects two of the five open problems, because it trades the most expensive software operation, per-element random numbers, for a Hadamard transform the quantizer already half-implements. The library's real bottleneck is therefore a quantizer roadmap plus an evaluation protocol trustworthy enough to rank 0.003-nat changes. Kernel scheduling and grouped-GEMM coverage matter mainly for users on different hardware or with MoE models, not for the core result.

The attention result shows a pattern the field is converging on. A quantized forward is only as good as the consistency of what the backward recomputes. The library's two-term P·V, a design reached by its own experiments, is a cheaper instance of the Attn-QAT pattern than the published version. Further attention work therefore pays off only through faster exponentials, not through a different gradient. The open scientific risk is the regime: at under one token per parameter, the library can show that a published recipe transfers, but it cannot rank recipes until the long-horizon run exists.
